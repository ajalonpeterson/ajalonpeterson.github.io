case study — GEOG 5543 final project

Predicting modern locations from a 1930s plat map, using nothing but OCR and a street buffer.

A scanned property plat has no coordinates, no metadata, and handwriting that shifts direction mid-street. This was an attempt to pull real street names off one and line them up against the modern city — using ordinary GIS tools to do most of the work an AI model usually gets credit for.

SourceRamsey County plat archive, scanned PDF
ToolsOpenCV, EasyOCR, GeoPandas, OSMnx, DBSCAN
StatusExploratory pilot, single-plat proof of concept
fig. 01the source: a 1930s subdivision plat, Saint Paul, MN

The problem

County archives hold thousands of these plats — hand-lettered survey documents scanned as flat PDFs. There's no shapefile, no coordinate reference, nothing structured about them at all. If you want to know what a plat like this corresponds to on a modern map, you're reading it by eye.

Throwing OCR at the whole page doesn't really work. Street names, block numbers, surveyor's notes, and legal boilerplate all sit in the same visual space, at every angle, in a font that was hand-drawn a century ago. Run OCR on the full image and it reads all of it indiscriminately — signatures, stamps, and surveyor notes included — which makes the output nearly as messy as not running OCR at all.

The goal here wasn't to solve that for every plat in the archive. It was to build one working pipeline, end to end, on a single plat — and be honest in the notebook about which parts of it would need rethinking to generalize.

The pipeline

Four stages, in order: clean the scan, isolate the geometry that matters, OCR only that region, then match whatever text comes out against a modern street network.

01

Minimal preprocessing

The plat comes in as a scanned PDF and gets converted to a grayscale image — deliberately light preprocessing, since anything more aggressive at this stage tends to destroy faint pencil linework before it's even been detected.

02

Isolating the streets

This is the part of the pipeline that took the most iteration. An adaptive threshold turns the scan into a binary ink mask, connected-component filtering strips out small noise, and a line segment detector picks out straight linework — filtered by length and by a margin around the page border, since the border and legend boxes were consistently mistaken for street lines.

The actual trick, and the one I'd point to as the core idea of this project: instead of trying to classify which lines were streets, I buffered every detected line by a fixed pixel radius and unioned the buffers together. That gives you a corridor around anything line-shaped on the page. Buffer distance was hand-tuned per plat — a bread-and-butter GIS operation, repurposed as a filter for an OCR problem instead of the usual spatial-proximity question.

Everything outside those corridors gets masked to white. What's left is mostly street labels and the linework immediately around them — legal text, stamps, and interior lot numbers are gone before OCR ever sees the image.

03

OCR, run for recall instead of precision

Even inside the masked region, EasyOCR missed labels that ran vertically or at an angle — a couple of the street names on this plat, including the ones running along the diagonal streets, weren't legible right-side-up. The fix was running OCR three times, rotating the image to 0°, 90°, and -90° each pass, and keeping every hit.

That produces a genuinely noisy result — duplicate detections, partial words, stray characters caught in the linework:

Rather than hard-coding rules to throw results out, each detection gets a weighted rank score — OCR confidence, the ratio of uppercase characters (plat street labels are consistently all-caps), and text size, in that order of priority. Everything above a rank threshold, with a minimum length to filter out stray characters, survives; duplicates from the multi-angle passes get collapsed down to the highest-confidence version. It's a much more forgiving approach than a strict rule set, and it's what actually pulled the real street names to the top of the list.

04

Matching against the modern map

With a clean list of street names in hand, OSMnx pulls the current street network for Saint Paul and filters it down to edges whose names match. From there, every matched street's endpoints get extracted and run through DBSCAN, clustering points that sit close together in real-world distance rather than relying on modern intersections — which, a century of reconstruction later, often don't exist in the same place anymore, or at all.

The densest cluster with the most distinct matched streets gets kept, outlier points get trimmed by distance from the cluster's centroid, and what's left gets wrapped in a padded bounding box — the pipeline's best estimate of where this 1930s plat sits on a present-day map.

What worked, and what I'd rethink

A few approaches got tried and dropped before landing on the pipeline above — worth naming, since the dead ends are as much a part of this as what worked:

  • OCR on the full, unmasked image, before the buffer approach — too much non-street text got picked up, and cleaning it afterward proved harder than filtering beforehand.
  • Appending "ST" / "AVE" suffixes to bare street names pulled from OCR — dropped once it became clear the street-matching step didn't actually need suffixes to work.
  • Identifying modern intersections as fixed reference points — many of the intersections on this plat no longer exist as such, which is part of why the pipeline uses street proximity clustering instead.
  • A handful of geocoding services, tried as a shortcut to the matching step — none handled century-old, partial street names well enough to be usable.

On the DBSCAN step specifically

This is the piece I'm least confident generalizes. It works for this plat, but the clustering parameters were tuned by hand, and a plat with a different street layout or a sparser modern match would likely need different values, if this approach holds up at all. It's a solid first attempt at a genuinely hard sub-problem — matching century-old, incomplete street data against a constantly-changing modern network — rather than a finished method.

Same caveat applies more broadly: this whole pipeline was tuned against one plat. The buffer distance, the OCR rank thresholds, the DBSCAN parameters — all of it would likely need to flex per-plat to hold up across an archive with wildly different handwriting, scan quality, and street layouts. This was built and documented as a proof of concept, not a production tool — and the notebook says so throughout.

Why this seems worth sharing

There's a real, growing interest in pulling structured information out of historical maps — not just plats, but survey records, fire insurance maps, and other archival GIS material that mostly exists as a scanned image and nothing else. It came up directly in a conversation my professor had with the Minnesota Department of Transportation, which was part of what pushed me to write this project up rather than let it sit in a class folder.

The most useful part of this project, honestly, might not be the pipeline itself — it's the demonstration that a problem framed as "AI/OCR" can be meaningfully narrowed using ordinary spatial reasoning first. The buffer step did more work than the OCR model did.
